Inside YovaSense

GPU infrastructure operations,
from signal to verified recovery.

YovaSense runs autonomous operations for GPU infrastructure. It collects telemetry from GPUs, hosts, networking, schedulers and workloads, correlates it into one incident, diagnoses the likely cause, runs the recovery actions your policy allows and verifies that the capacity is healthy again.

The result: more healthy, usable GPU capacity from the fleet you already run, and less manual investigation for your GPU infrastructure operations team. Built for owned or rented clusters running training and inference, on NVIDIA and AMD.

How it works

An engineer in the loop.
Not an engineer on every alert.

  1. 01

    Collect

    Ingests GPU metrics, Xid and ECC errors, host and kernel logs, NIC and switch counters, scheduler state and job telemetry. Missing or stale sources are recorded as missing, never filled in.

  2. 02

    Correlate

    Aligns events in time and maps every job to its GPUs, hosts, NICs, switches and racks, so one fault becomes one incident instead of a dozen alerts.

  3. 03

    Diagnose

    Combines known failure signatures with AI reasoning to rank likely causes, each with the evidence for and against it. A cause stays a hypothesis until a check confirms it.

  4. 04

    Remediate

    Executes policy-approved recovery actions: GPU resets, service recovery, node isolation or workload rescheduling. Actions outside policy wait for a person's approval, and a safety limit caps how much capacity it takes out of service on its own.

  5. 05

    Verify

    Checks hardware health, scheduler availability and workload recovery before it closes an incident. If the capacity did not come back, the incident stays open.

  6. 06

    Escalate

    Opens a prioritized ticket with the component's identity, the evidence and repair instructions for anything that needs a technician.

Research and engineering

Where YovaSense comes from.

YovaSense started with one question about distributed training: which GPU is slowing this job down? Answering it meant building a straggler detector, a mixed-vendor training backend and an RDMA control channel, and measuring them on real NVIDIA and AMD GPUs. That work is below. Straggler detection is now one part of the performance degradation family.

01Research · stragglers

Nothing crashes. Every step just gets slower.

Synchronous training moves in lockstep. Each step ends in a collective, such as the all-reduce that averages gradients, and a collective finishes only when the last rank arrives. One slow rank, often called a straggler, sets the pace for every GPU in the job while the rest sit idle at the barrier.

When that slowdown starts partway through a run, it's a performance regression. Throughput drops a few percent, the loss still goes down, utilization still looks busy, and nothing points at the GPU, link or host that changed.

Where regressions come from

  • Power, clocks, configuration

    A power cap, clocks that stop boosting, a driver or setting that changed.

  • Thermal

    A GPU that throttles as it heats up.

  • Communication

    A degraded port, link or switch path, or one island's collectives slowing down.

  • Host and input

    A data loader, CPU or storage stall that holds one rank back off the GPU.

What it costs

s ÷ (1 + s)

If one rank's compute runs s slower, every step stretches by up to s, and up to s ÷ (1 + s) of every GPU-hour in the job goes to waiting: the bound when compute sets the step. One rank 8% slow in a 512-GPU job can idle the equivalent of 38 GPUs.

02How it came together

One question led to three layers of infrastructure.

Which GPU is slowing this job down? Answering that across NVIDIA and AMD meant building the layer underneath first.

  1. 01The question

    The detector idea

    Find the GPU that's slowing a distributed job while the job is still running, on NVIDIA and AMD alike, instead of reading timings after the run is paid for.

  2. 02The prerequisite

    One job, two vendors

    Detecting across a mixed fleet needs a job that spans it, and NCCL and RCCL can't exchange data. PyTorch's "nccl" backend runs NCCL on CUDA builds and RCCL on ROCm builds, never both in one job.

  3. 03Open source · MIT

    GPUBridge, in Python

    A PyTorch backend that keeps each vendor on its native library and joins them over a Gloo bridge. It went on to train DDP, FSDP2 and pipeline-parallel jobs across an NVIDIA V100 and an AMD MI50.

    Read more
  4. 04Rust · libibverbs

    From Gloo to Rust and RDMA

    Measurements put the bottleneck on the bridge: about 1.2 GB/s of TCP through host memory. Research into RDMA moved the transports to Rust. A multi-stream TCP prototype missed its own speed target and was dropped; a raw-verbs transport over libibverbs followed and has run on InfiniBand hardware, between two NVIDIA islands on one host.

    Read more
  5. 05The detector

    YovaSense

    With mixed-vendor jobs running, the detector followed. It flags regressions, estimates what each one costs, suggests a likely cause and, on opt-in, rebalances work at runtime. Its agent, coordinator and scoring core are Rust, with their own RDMA channel.

    Read more

    Fleet operations

    The detector became one signal in a larger loop. YovaSense now collects, correlates, diagnoses, remediates, verifies and escalates infrastructure incidents across GPUs, hosts, networking, schedulers and workloads.

03GPUBridge · open source · MIT

NVIDIA and AMD GPUs in one PyTorch job.

A PyTorch install is a CUDA build or a ROCm build, so each process speaks NCCL or RCCL, and an NCCL communicator can't exchange data with an RCCL one. GPUBridge is a torch.distributed backend that joins them.

Each vendor's GPUs form an island on their native library. For an all-reduce, each island reduces onto its leader, the leaders exchange over the bridge, and each leader broadcasts the result back. Everything inside an island stays on NCCL or RCCL; only the leaders' exchange crosses vendors.

It composes collectives PyTorch already provides, so it runs on stock CUDA and ROCm builds over any network, and moving a script over is one import and one string.

Read the codeOpen an issue
  • DDP, FSDP2 and HSDP through torch.distributed
  • Async collectives and a DDP communication hook
  • Pipeline parallel across vendors, with automatic stage balancing
  • Optional bf16 and fp8 compression on the bridge, in Triton kernels
  • Swappable bridge transports: Gloo by default, Rust verbs opt-in
  • A simulation mode that runs two vendors on one CPU
  • 2.75×faster 1 GB all-reduce between a V100 and an MI50 with lossy fp8 on the bridge, 1.67 s to 0.61 s
  • 1.10×a V100's training throughput, with an MI50 added as a second pipeline stage (best hand-tuned split; automatic gave 1.07×)
  • 6GPU models it has run on: V100, T4, L40S, A100, H200 and MI50

On one V100 and one MI50 over TCP, the pipeline beat the V100 alone and data parallelism didn't. Each data-parallel step all-reduces 536 MB of gradients over a link of about 1.2 GB/s; the pipeline passes 3 MB of activations per microbatch. Mixing vendors pays when the parallelism fits the link.

The terms, precisely

NCCL and RCCL
NVIDIA's and AMD's collective communication libraries. Within one vendor they choose the fastest path themselves: NVLink or xGMI inside a node, InfiniBand or RoCE between nodes.
Gloo
PyTorch's CPU collective backend, over TCP. It's GPUBridge's default bridge because it runs on any network.
InfiniBand
The fabric: host channel adapters (HCAs) and switches built for low-latency, high-bandwidth traffic between machines.
RDMA and verbs
RDMA lets a network adapter read and write registered memory directly, with no copy through the remote CPU. libibverbs, from rdma-core, is the user-space API both Rust transports call.

05Research · the straggler detector

From collective timings to a finding you can act on.

Every rank already checks in at the same place several times a step: the collectives. YovaSense times them on each rank, scores each window of steps against the rank's peers and its own history, and reports what slowed down, by how much and why. Collection never blocks a rank, and scoring happens away from the training step.

Source private
  1. 01

    Observe

    Each rank times its own compute gap between collectives and its wait inside them. The late rank has the longest gap and the shortest wait. Each rank measures only its own durations, so no clocks need syncing across nodes.

  2. 02

    Score

    Each window, every rank is compared with the other ranks in its island: about 10% over their median makes it suspect, and 3 suspect windows out of the last 4 flag it. Each island's warmup baseline catches a whole island slowing together. Opt-in hardware classes keep a slower GPU generation from reading as a fault.

  3. 03

    Explain

    Each finding carries its cost in milliseconds and percent of the step, and a likely cause from GPU telemetry: thermal, power, clocks, PCIe or host. Missing telemetry is reported as missing. A hang names the ranks behind and dumps each rank's last 256 collectives.

  4. 04Opt-in

    Act

    Rebalancing moves batch work between the ranks the job already has, in proportion to measured speed and within set bounds. The global batch stays the same and each gradient is weighted by its samples, so the update is the mean over every sample, as before.

02 · Policy and bounds

A policy file you write sets the global batch and each rank's bounds. The imbalance must be at least 15% per sample, and the predicted gain at least 3% of the step. Each plan moves only half way toward the target.

07 · Scored again, per sample

Each rank logs its batch from the commit on, so the scorer compares time per sample. A rank given more samples isn't read as slowing down, and rank 2 is still the slow GPU per sample. The step gets shorter because no one waits as long.

One plan at a time, 4 windows between plans counted from the commit, and at most 6 per run. A later plan, such as the way back once the rank recovers, needs only the measured gain and change.

07Diagnosis

How YovaSense ranks the cause.

Finding the failing component is half the job. The other half is knowing what to check and what to do about it.

Known failure signatures

  • Every incident becomes a versioned record: what was flagged, the GPUs and software involved, and measurements from before and after.
  • Likely causes are ranked across the six incident families: GPU hardware failures, network and interconnect, performance degradation, drivers and services, unavailable GPU nodes, and power and cooling. They're ordered by the kind of evidence behind them, never by an invented probability.
  • Missing or stale evidence is listed as missing, and competing causes stay on the record.
  • Each record suggests the next check that would separate the leading causes, then tracks the fix until healthy, usable GPU capacity is back.

AI reasoning, trained on incidents

  • An LLM, trained or adapted on permitted, labeled GPU incident and recovery records.
  • It suggests likely causes, the checks that would confirm them and remedies, citing the evidence it used.
  • It is judged on held-out, resolved incidents, and its suggestions stay hypotheses until the evidence confirms them.

The interactive demo walks through signature-based diagnosis on a simulated fleet, with a scripted model standing in for the AI.
