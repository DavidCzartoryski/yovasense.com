Beyond monitoring

Monitoring raises the alert. YovaSense drives it to resolution.

Monitoring tells an operator that a GPU disappeared or a job failed. The evidence for why is spread across the scheduler, the host, the GPU, the fabric and the job, and an engineer still connects it by hand.

What monitoring tells you

  • A node is drained.
  • A GPU stopped reporting.
  • A link is flapping.
  • A job failed.

What YovaSense adds

  • One incident instead of four alerts.
  • A named GPU and a likely cause.
  • Capacity back in the scheduler, checked.
  • A technician ticket only when needed.

How it works

From infrastructure failure to verified recovery.

One loop for every incident, across GPUs, hosts, networking, schedulers, storage and workloads. YovaSense fixes what your policy allows and sends the rest to a person with the evidence attached.

  1. 01

    Collect

    Ingests GPU metrics, Xid and ECC errors, host logs, NIC and switch counters, scheduler state and job telemetry.

    DCGM · kernel log · NIC and switch counters · Slurm · Kubernetes · job telemetry

  2. 02

    Correlate

    Aligns events in time and maps every job to its GPUs, hosts, NICs, switches and racks, so one fault becomes one incident, not a page of alerts.

    job → GPU → host → NIC → switch → rack

  3. 03

    Diagnose

    Combines known failure signatures with AI reasoning to rank likely causes, and shows the evidence for and against each one.

    failure signatures · AI reasoning · evidence

  4. 04

    Remediate

    Executes policy-approved recovery actions: GPU resets, service recovery, node isolation or workload rescheduling. Anything outside policy waits for your approval.

    reset GPU · restart service · drain node · reschedule job

  5. 05

    Verify

    Checks hardware health, scheduler availability and workload recovery before it closes an incident, so what comes back is healthy, usable GPU capacity.

    hardware health · back in the scheduler · job at normal pace

  6. 06 · branch

    Escalate

    Opens a prioritized ticket with the component's identity, the evidence and repair instructions for anything that needs a technician.

    → An engineer in the loop. Not an engineer on every alert.

Cross-layer correlation

A GPU node becomes unavailable.

Four systems each see part of the failure. None of them connects it to the job, the node and the fix.

  1. Scheduler

    Slurm reports that the node is drained.

  2. Kubernetes

    The GPU device plugin reports seven GPUs instead of eight.

  3. Host kernel

    The kernel log reports NVIDIA Xid 79: the GPU has fallen off the bus.

  4. PCIe

    PCIe telemetry shows the GPU is no longer reachable.

  5. YovaSense

    YovaSense correlates the four signals into one incident on one GPU. It isolates the node, restarts the affected job on healthy GPUs from its last checkpoint, and decides whether a controlled recovery attempt is appropriate or the GPU needs hardware inspection. The node returns to service only after it passes its health checks; if it needs hands, the technician gets a ticket with the node, the GPU slot, the evidence and the repair steps.

Second example

A degraded InfiniBand link slows healthy GPUs.

Every GPU passes its health checks, and the job still runs slow. One switch port is logging symbol errors and link retrains, so every collective that crosses it waits. GPU dashboards show idle time. The fabric shows a rising counter. YovaSense maps the job's ranks to their NICs and switch ports, finds the one link they share, reschedules the work away from it and opens a ticket to reseat or replace the cable. Then it checks that the job is back at its normal pace.

The interactive demo shows a harder network fault: a leaf switch fails, and YovaSense stops draining nodes at its safety limit and hands the decision to a person (story 11 of 12).

What it handles

Six families of infrastructure incidents.

Built for GPU infrastructure operations teams running training and inference. The same loop runs for every family.

  • GPU hardware failures

    Xid errors, ECC faults, GPUs that fall off the bus.

  • Network and interconnect

    RDMA failures, NCCL timeouts, NVLink degradation.

  • Performance degradation

    Training stragglers, throttling, throughput regressions.

  • Drivers and services

    CUDA, GPU drivers, Fabric Manager, firmware.

  • Unavailable GPU nodes

    Slurm drains, Kubernetes health checks, stranded capacity.

  • Power and cooling

    Thermal throttling, rack-level power and cooling faults.

Research and engineering

Measured on real GPUs.

YovaSense grew out of GPU reliability research on Northeastern's Explorer cluster. That work produced a straggler detector, tested on NVIDIA V100s with InfiniBand, and GPUBridge, an open-source PyTorch backend that runs NVIDIA and AMD GPUs in one job. Straggler detection is now one part of the performance degradation family.

  • YovaSense · 4×V100

    8 of 8

    injected slowdowns of about 10% and 20% flagged on the right rank, on one node of four V100s, with a tuned policy profile

  • YovaSense · 4×V100

    +0.01%

    median change in step time with full detection on, across 9 matched pairs

  • YovaSense · 4×V100

    +34.9%

    median samples per second after rebalancing work away from a slowed rank, across 3 matched pairs (a small MLP with one rank slowed by injected work)

  • GPUBridge · V100 + MI50

    2.75×

    faster 1 GB all-reduce over TCP between a V100 and an MI50 with GPUBridge, using lossy fp8 on the bridge (1.67 s to 0.61 s)

Counts are cases, not accuracy rates. GPUBridge has run on six GPU models: V100, T4, L40S, A100, H200 and MI50.
